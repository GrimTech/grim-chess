# Kotov's "Think Like a Grandmaster": The Tree of Analysis and Its Critiques

## 1. The method

Alexander Kotov (1913–1981), a Soviet GM and author, set out a systematic calculation procedure in *Think Like a Grandmaster* (English edition 1971) [1]:

1. **Identify all candidate moves** in the position before analysing any of them.
2. **Analyse each candidate in turn**, building a **tree of analysis** of branches and sub-branches, examining each branch **only once**, without jumping back and forth.
3. **Evaluate the end positions** of each branch and choose the move whose worst-case outcome is best [1][4].

Kotov also warned against habits such as circular thinking (re-analysing the same line repeatedly), assessing positions without concrete calculation, and mismanaging time [2].

**Kotov syndrome.** Kotov described a player who calculates at length in a complex position, finds nothing convincing, runs short of time and then plays a move they have hardly analysed, which turns out to be a blunder [2][3]. The term is now widely used.

## 2. Why the strict method is rejected

### 2.1 Expertise research
De Groot's think-aloud protocols showed that strong players use **progressive deepening**. They return to the same line repeatedly, extending and revising it as new information appears [8]. This contradicts "analyse each branch only once." Pattern recognition supplies the *first* candidates. Others emerge only during analysis (see `../01-learning-science/pattern-recognition-and-expertise.md`).

### 2.2 Practitioner critiques
- **Nunn** (*Secrets of Practical Chess*, 1998) argued that players do not and should not calculate this way. He advocated a quick scan of tempting and forcing moves first, followed by a careful candidate list only in complicated positions [4][5][9].
- **Tisdall** (*Improve Your Chess Now*, 1997) also criticized the rigid tree and argued that calculation and evaluation should be integrated rather than run as separate stages [9].
- **Dvoretsky and Aagaard** kept the *candidate-moves* principle but stressed searching for the **opponent's resources** (prophylactic thinking), comparing lines and allowing deliberate backtracking [4][7].
- **GM Bryan Smith's review**: candidate moves often become apparent only *after* some variations have been examined. The method omits hypothesis testing and backtracking, and pattern recognition matters more than organized procedure [2].
- **Leitão** showed concretely that Kotov's own illustrative analysis misses stronger candidates. The no-return rule prevented applying an idea found in one branch to an earlier branch [4].

**Consensus:** "Candidate moves" is valuable advice, especially against tunnel vision and the Einstellung effect. The full tree with its "only once" rule is an idealization that no strong player follows. *Expert consensus; no controlled studies.*

## 3. What replaced it: practical calculation procedures

A composite of Nunn, Aagaard, Dvoretsky and Heisman, as used in modern training [4][5][7]:

1. **Quick scan:** checks, captures and threats for both sides (the "forcing moves first" rule).
2. **Strategic orientation:** what are the weaknesses, what is my worst-placed piece, and what does my opponent want? (Aagaard's "three questions"; see `aagaard-grandmaster-preparation.md`).
3. **Candidate list** in complex positions, including at least one non-obvious move (anti-Einstellung).
4. **Calculate with permission to return.** Revise the candidate list when new ideas appear.
5. **Look for the opponent's best reply**, including quiet moves and in-between moves (Dvoretsky's emphasis).
6. **Evaluate end positions** concretely (material, king safety, activity), not "unclear."
7. **Time budget.** Commit after a limit (e.g., Leitão suggests a 15-minute training limit) to avoid Kotov syndrome [4].
8. **Blunder check** of the chosen move before playing it (see `heisman-thought-process-and-real-chess.md`).

## 4. Trainable sub-skills

| Sub-skill | Training format | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate generation breadth | "List all candidates" exercises scored against an engine's top-N | Anti-tunnel-vision |
| Opponent resource detection | "Find Black's best defence" exercises | Dvoretsky emphasis |
| Depth and visualization | Multi-move lines entered without moving pieces | Builds tolerance for working-memory load |
| Evaluation of end positions | "Evaluate this position at the end of the line" | Calculation without evaluation is useless |
| Time management | Timed exercises with commitment | Prevents Kotov syndrome |

## 5. Implications for Grim Chess

1. **Do not teach the strict Kotov tree.** Teach the composite procedure (§3) and present Kotov historically.
2. **Calculation exercises require full-line entry** before feedback, scored on the main line *and* the critical defences found.
3. **"Candidate breadth" metric.** In thinking-process training mode, ask learners to enter their candidates first and compare them with the engine's top moves. Track over time how often the best move was not on the list (a missing-candidate rate).
4. **Kotov-syndrome detection** in the learner's games: a long think (> X% of remaining time), followed by a quickly played different move that blunders. Surface it in post-game reports.
5. **Timed "commit" mode** in calculation drills, to build decision discipline.

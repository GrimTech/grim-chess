# Aagaard's Grandmaster Preparation Series

## 1. Author and aim

Jacob Aagaard (b. 1973) is a Danish-Scottish GM (IM 1997, GM 2007), 2007 British Champion, co-founder of Quality Chess and a prominent trainer. He describes Mark Dvoretsky as his mentor [7][8]. The **Grandmaster Preparation** series (Quality Chess, 2012–2014) has **six volumes** and runs to more than 2,000 pages [2][3]:

1. **Calculation**
2. **Positional Play**
3. **Strategic Play**
4. **Attack & Defence**
5. **Endgame Play**
6. **Thinking Inside the Box** (added later, unifying the previous five) [2][3]

Aagaard's stated goal is "to show a path towards playing chess at grandmaster level for those who do not have access to a good trainer." He notes that the series title is partly a joke, because the original five-book plan contains no opening coverage [1].

## 2. Calculation (volume 1)

The table of contents defines the calculation toolkit [1]:

| Chapter | Technique |
|---|---|
| Concentration, Concentration, Concentration | Preliminary: the effort required |
| Calculation Theory in 60 Seconds | Overview |
| 1. Candidate Moves | Looking for options first. Aagaard keeps Kotov's insight but explicitly rejects his "land of trees and mechanical thinking" [1] |
| 2. Combinational Vision | Seeing tactical patterns |
| 3. Prophylaxis | Opponent's ideas (Dvoretsky lineage) |
| 4. Comparison | Choosing between similar lines by identifying the difference |
| 5. Elimination | Discarding candidates to reach a decision |
| 6. Intermediate Moves | Zwischenzugs, often the overlooked resource |
| 7. Imagination | Unusual and counter-intuitive moves |
| 8. Traps | Practical traps |
| 9. Tests / 10. Difficult Positions | Mixed exam sets |

Aagaard's core principle, illustrated with a Tikkanen game: "all calculation techniques are based on looking for moves we have not seen immediately" [1]. This is an explicit **anti-Einstellung** philosophy (see `../01-learning-science/pattern-recognition-and-expertise.md`).

## 3. Positional Play and Strategic Play (volumes 2–3)

**Positional Play** is built around **three questions** for positions without immediate tactics (as widely summarized by readers and trainers [5]):
1. **Where are the weaknesses?**
2. **Which is the worst-placed piece?**
3. **What is my opponent's idea?** (prophylaxis)

**Strategic Play** extends this to long-term planning and decision-making in complex strategic positions [2][5].

## 4. Attack & Defence, Endgame Play, Thinking Inside the Box

- **Attack & Defence:** principles of attacking (bring more pieces, open lines, prophylaxis against defensive resources) and of defending (calm, accurate, resourceful). This builds on Aagaard's earlier *Practical Chess Defence* [1][5].
- **Endgame Play:** practical endgame decision-making rather than theoretical positions. It complements Dvoretsky's Endgame Manual (see `endgame-study-methods.md`) [5].
- **Thinking Inside the Box:** decision-making as a whole: when to calculate, how to play simple positions, what calculation is, and practical time use [2]. The Quality Chess blog's "The 5%" post discusses the idea that a small share of critical moments decides most games, and gives advice on how to read the series [4].

## 5. How the books are meant to be used

From the author's introduction and publisher guidance [1][4]:
- **Solve as in a game.** Use a board without moving the pieces, time each exercise and write down full variations before checking.
- **The exercises are hard.** They are designed for players aiming at about 2300–2600. Reviewers suggest that some material can help players from about 1600–2000 if they are prepared to struggle [5]. *Expertise-reversal logic suggests they will overwhelm most sub-1700 players (see `../01-learning-science/cognitive-load-theory.md`).*
- **Groups and trainers.** The exercises suit study groups that compare solutions.

## 6. Critiques

- **Difficulty.** Many readers find the exercises too hard and become demotivated. This is a calibration problem.
- **Engine-era rigor vs human practicality.** Some critics argue that very deep engine-verified solutions blur practical decision-making. Aagaard argues the opposite.
- **No outcome data.** Endorsements come from strong players and trainers. Given Aagaard's role, the many testimonials from Quality Chess's own authors are potential conflicts of interest.

## 7. What Grim Chess should adopt

1. **The calculation technique taxonomy** (candidates, combinational vision, prophylaxis, comparison, elimination, intermediate moves, imagination) as **tags on calculation exercises**. Report learner performance by technique. That makes the diagnosis actionable: for example, "you miss intermediate moves."
2. **The three questions** as an optional prompt in analysis mode and in positional exercises.
3. **"Exam mode"** for advanced learners: no piece moving, a timer and full-line entry. Score on the main line plus key defences.
4. **Difficulty gating.** GM Preparation-level material unlocks at about 1900+ by default (with an override). Below that level, use Yusupov or Steps-level equivalents (see `yusupov-nine-book-series.md`).
5. **"Critical moments" training** (the 5% idea). The platform detects high-stakes moments in the learner's own games (large eval swings, sharp positions) and turns them into exercises.
6. **Licensing candidate.** The series is high quality for an "advanced track". Engine-verify the solutions on ingest.

# Endgame Study Methods

## 1. Why endgames are special for training

- **A finite, stable canon.** A modest set of theoretical positions (Lucena, Philidor, Vancura, key squares, opposition, triangulation, Q vs advanced pawn, and so on) recurs across all levels. They do not go out of date like openings.
- **Ground-truth feedback exists.** Endgame tablebases (Syzygy, up to 7 pieces) give perfect evaluations and optimal moves [8]. Feedback quality is therefore higher than in any other phase.
- **Skill vs knowledge.** Practical endgame play (technique, converting advantages, defending worse positions) differs from knowing theoretical positions. Both must be trained.

## 2. The main texts and their roles

### 2.1 Silman's Complete Endgame Course (2007): rating-banded sequencing
Silman organizes endgame material by **rating class**: a beginner section (up to about 999), then bands of about 200 points (Class E 1000–1199, D 1200–1399, C 1400–1599, B 1600–1799, A 1800–1999, Expert 2000–2199, Master 2200–2399), and finally material for advanced players [1][2][3]. The principle is to learn **only what you need at your level** and postpone rarer endings (e.g., B+N vs K) until they are likely to matter.
- Examples: Class E covers basic king and pawn endings, opposition, and minor piece vs queen basics. Class D continues pawn endings and piece vs pawn [2].
- *Strength:* an explicit, level-appropriate curriculum. *Weakness:* the band boundaries are judgment-based, and reviewers note that some sections are light on exercises [3].

### 2.2 de la Villa, *100 Endgames You Must Know* (2008; updated editions; workbook)
GM Jesús de la Villa selects about 100 essential theoretical positions, organized by material type, with a final test, written in a lively and accessible style [4][5][7]. Reviewers disagree on the target: some recommend it for club players after the basics, others say it is really for about 2000+ players filling gaps [3][5]. A companion workbook supplies practice exercises.

### 2.3 Dvoretsky's Endgame Manual (2003; later editions)
Comprehensive, deep and demanding. Essential material is highlighted in **blue text** (the core canon), and the rest is advanced or professional-level [6]. Reviewers call it the book with "all the theoretical knowledge you would need as a professional player," and some find it hard to read [6][7]. Common advice is de la Villa first, then Dvoretsky [7].

### 2.4 Practical endgame skill
- **Shereshevsky, *Endgame Strategy*** (1985): principles of technique (do not hurry, centralize the king, create a second weakness, schematic thinking).
- **Aagaard, *GM Preparation: Endgame Play*** (see `aagaard-grandmaster-preparation.md`).
- **Müller & Lamprecht, *Fundamental Chess Endings*** (2001): an encyclopedic reference.
- **Adult-improver evidence (anecdotal).** Jonathan Hawkins, who went from about 1800 in his late teens to GM, attributed much of his rise to deep endgame study, documented in *Amateur to IM* [9].

### 2.5 Composed studies for calculation
Elite Indian coach GM R.B. Ramesh advocates **solving composed endgame studies** (e.g., hours of studies as a demanding calculation routine) for imagination and precise calculation [10]. Studies are hard and suited to about 1800+.

## 3. A sequenced canon (platform default)

The order below is an expert-consensus synthesis of [1][4][6]. Band assignments follow Silman's principle but are platform defaults to be validated.

| Band (approx.) | Content |
|---|---|
| 0–999 | K+Q vs K, K+R vs K (and 2R vs K) mates; rule of the square; basic K+P vs K with opposition |
| 1000–1399 | Key squares; opposition and outflanking; K+P vs K complete; simple pawn races; Q vs pawn on the 7th (basic) |
| 1400–1799 | Lucena and Philidor; basic rook endings (rook behind passed pawn); triangulation; outside passed pawn; good vs bad bishop basics; knight vs pawn |
| 1800–2199 | Vancura; R+P vs R deeper (short-side/long-side defence); Q vs R basics; opposite-colored bishops; B vs N principles; fortress concepts; R vs minor piece |
| 2200+ | Dvoretsky core (blue text) in full; rook + multiple pawns; Q endings; B+N vs K; practical technique (Shereshevsky); composed studies |

## 4. Training formats

| Format | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Explain → demonstrate → play out vs tablebase** | Theoretical positions | The learner plays the winning (or drawing) side against perfect defence, with tablebase feedback on each move (distance to mate or zeroing). The highest-fidelity drill available |
| **Spaced repetition of the canon** | Retention | Schedule position "cards" with play-out recall rather than single-move recall (see `../01-learning-science/spaced-repetition-and-fsrs.md`) |
| **Discrimination pairs** | Confusable positions | E.g., Philidor vs Lucena, won vs drawn K+P configurations (interleaving benefit for similar categories) |
| **Practical endgame play-outs** | Technique | Play out non-theoretical endgames from master games against an engine at calibrated strength. Score conversion rate |
| **Composed studies** | Calculation and imagination (1800+) | Full-solution entry before feedback |
| **Own-game endgame review** | Diagnosis | Flag endgames in which the learner's result was worse than the tablebase or engine evaluation (lost wins, lost draws) |

## 5. Evidence notes

- **Capablanca's advice to "study endgames first,"** often repeated by coaches, has no empirical test. A reasonable rationale is that endgames teach piece activity and calculation in low-complexity positions, which also reduces cognitive load.
- **No controlled studies** compare endgame-first and tactics-first curricula. The platform should run one: randomize the sequencing of endgame vs tactics emphasis for new 1200–1600 learners and compare 6-month rating change. This is ethically low-risk, since both arms are reasonable.

## 6. Implications for Grim Chess

1. Ship the **rating-banded endgame canon** (§3) with tablebase-backed play-out drills from day one.
2. **Mastery = play-out success against tablebase defence**, not passive reading.
3. SRS-schedule the canon with **high retention targets** (about 0.9), given its stability and frequency.
4. Compute an **endgame conversion metric** from learners' games and use it in diagnosis (see `../01-learning-science/plateaus-and-rating-stagnation.md`).
5. Consider licensing de la Villa (club band) and Dvoretsky (advanced band) content. Otherwise build an equivalent canon from public-domain theory with tablebase verification.

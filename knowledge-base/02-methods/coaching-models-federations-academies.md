# Coaching Models of Federations and Academies

## 1. FIDE Trainers' Commission (TRG)

- FIDE recognizes **five trainer titles**: FIDE Senior Trainer (FST), FIDE Trainer (FT), FIDE Instructor (FI), National Instructor (NI) and Developmental Instructor (DI) [1].
- TRG regulates titles and licences, runs **FIDE Trainer Seminars** and professional-development workshops, and maintains FIDE-Endorsed Academies [1].
- Historical requirements for higher titles included national trainer education, federation recommendation, years of training activity and successful seminar participation. Licence terms and fees have changed across regulation versions [1]. *Check the current handbook chapter before relying on specifics.*
- **What it standardizes:** trainer credentials and minimum knowledge, not a single mandated curriculum. Seminar content varies by event.

**Implication:** for platform coach-marketplace vetting, FIDE trainer titles are a useful **minimum credential** signal, alongside verified playing strength and safeguarding checks (see `../05-children/online-safety-for-minors.md`).

## 2. National-federation curricula: Netherlands (Steps)

The Royal Dutch Chess Federation adopted the **Steps Method** as its official teaching method. It is a federation-level, curriculum-driven model with graded certificates per Step, used across clubs and schools (see `steps-method.md`) [13]. It is the best example of a **standardized national beginner curriculum**.

## 3. US Chess School (elite talent camps)

- Founded by **Greg Shahade in 2006** [2].
- **Free, intensive, in-person camps of about five days** for top juniors, usually **8–12 students**, led by strong GMs and admitted by rating threshold (e.g., one 2014 listing required 2275+ for a general camp and 1900+ for a girls' camp). Held around 3 times a year in New York, St. Louis, Seattle, Dallas and San Francisco [2][3].
- The school states that many alumni became GMs and age-group world champions, and that all 18 players in the 2013 US Junior Closed and Cadet championships had attended [2]. *Self-reported. Selection effects make causal attribution impossible.*

**Model:** periodic, high-intensity, small-group exposure to elite trainers plus a peer cohort, layered on top of each player's ongoing private coaching.

## 4. India's surge

### 4.1 Scale
India had **3 GMs before 2000** and about **85–88 by 2024–25**. Tamil Nadu supplies about a third, and one Chennai school group (Velammal) alone accounts for well over a dozen [4][5][6].

### 4.2 Ecosystem features (reported)
- **Dense academy market**: more than 60 recognized academies in Chennai alone, with after-school training as a mainstream activity [5][6].
- **School programmes** that recruit, sponsor and give academic flexibility to chess talents (Velammal) [6].
- **Role-model effect**: Viswanathan Anand's success from the late 1980s, and later Gukesh and Praggnanandhaa, cited by coaches as cultural drivers [4][6].
- **Long tournament calendar** with many FIDE-rated opens in India, giving rating and norm opportunities (reported; widely noted).
- **Elite mentoring**: the **WestBridge-Anand Chess Academy (WACA)**, launched in late 2020 as a funded fellowship. The initial fellows were Praggnanandhaa, Nihal Sarin, Raunak Sadhwani, Gukesh and Vaishali (Arjun Erigaisi was added later), with founding mentors including Artur Yusupov, Boris Gelfand, Grzegorz Gajewski and Sandipan Chanda. Work was initially online because of COVID [7][8].
- **Foreign elite camps**: e.g., Vladimir Kramnik's 2019 camp for six Indian teenagers, including Gukesh and Praggnanandhaa (organized by ChessBase India and sponsored by Microsense). It focused on positional and psychological handling, opponent-specific opening preparation and patient play [12].

### 4.3 Documented training habits of the top two
- **D. Gukesh** (coach GM Vishnu Prasanna, 2017–2022): very high training volume with few breaks, **largely engine-free training** until near elite level, and several hours a day with the coach nearby [9]. World Champion in December 2024.
- **R. Praggnanandhaa** (coach GM R.B. Ramesh, Chess Gurukul): about **4–6+ hours a day** of chess work at the academy [10], calculation and imagination training with composed studies, and pre-event camps combining about **8 hours of chess, 3–4 hours of sport and about 10 hours of sleep** (2024 Candidates prep) [11].

### 4.4 Causality caveat
The surge coincides with population size, rising middle-class investment, online chess growth (especially during COVID), role models, academy competition and tournament supply. **No study isolates the contribution of any training method.** Survivorship bias applies to the "habits of champions" narratives.

## 5. Common structural features across successful systems

| Feature | Soviet | Netherlands | USA (USCS) | India | Platform-substitutable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Large participation base | Yes | Moderate | Moderate | Yes | **Partly** (online reach) |
| Standardized beginner curriculum | Yes | **Yes (Steps)** | No | Varies by academy | **Yes** |
| Long-term individual coach | Yes | For talents | Yes (private) | **Yes** | Partly (coach marketplace, AI guidance) |
| Periodic elite camps | Yes | — | **Yes** | Yes (WACA, Kramnik) | Partly (online cohorts) |
| Peer cohort of similar strength | Yes | Clubs | **Yes** | Academies | **Yes** (study groups, leagues) |
| Dense OTB tournament supply | Yes | Yes | Moderate | **Yes** | **No** |
| Diagnosis-driven training | **Yes (Dvoretsky)** | Partly | Varies | Yes | **Yes** (data-driven) |
| Physical and wellness regimen | Botvinnik | — | — | Ramesh camps | Partly |

## 6. Implications for Grim Chess

1. **Curriculum standardization** (Steps-like) for beginners, and **diagnosis-driven personalization** (Dvoretsky-like) above that.
2. **Coach marketplace** vetted by credentials (FIDE titles, rating) and **mandatory safeguarding** (background checks where legally possible, no private messaging with minors; see `../05-children/online-safety-for-minors.md`).
3. **Cohorts:** rating-matched study groups and online "camps" of 1–2 weeks with structured daily programmes, modelled on USCS and WACA.
4. **OTB pathway guidance:** tournament calendars and norm-planning guidance for advanced users, because the platform cannot replace OTB events.
5. **Honesty:** do not attribute national or elite outcomes to any single method in marketing.

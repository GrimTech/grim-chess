# Starting Age, Cognitive Ability and Other "Talent" Factors in Chess

Deliberate practice explains roughly a third of the reliable variance in chess skill (see `deliberate-practice-in-chess.md`). This file reviews the main candidates for the rest.

## 1. Starting age

**Findings:**
- In Gobet & Campitelli's Argentine sample, mean age of starting serious play was 18.6 for unrated players, 14.2 for rated non-titled players, 11.6 for FMs, 10.3 for IMs and 11.3 for GMs [1].
- **Starting age correlated with rating even after controlling for accumulated practice** (partial r = −.40) [1][2].
- The probability of reaching IM/GM level was about **1 in 4 for players who started seriously before 13**, versus about **1 in 55 for those who started at 13 or later** [1][2].
- Burgoyne et al.'s (2019) structural-equation reanalysis of the large Charness et al. dataset found that serious starting age **still predicted peak rating (β = −.15) after controlling for latent serious chess activity and instruction** [2].
- Howard (2012) reported a similar independent starting-age effect [3].

**Interpretation:** this pattern is consistent with a **sensitive period**, in which early exposure produces more efficient chunk and template acquisition, as proposed for language. Alternatives cannot be ruled out:
- *Selection:* children who start early may be the ones who showed early aptitude or had chess-playing parents.
- *Practice measurement error:* if early-life practice is under-reported, starting age may partly proxy for unmeasured hours.
- *Quality of practice:* early starters may have had better coaching.

**Evidence strength: moderate.** The findings replicate across independent datasets, but all are correlational.

**What it does *not* mean:** late starters cannot improve dramatically. Adult improvers reaching master and even GM level exist (see `trajectories-and-hours-to-titles.md`). The base rates are simply much lower.

## 2. General cognitive ability (intelligence)

**Burgoyne et al. (2016) meta-analysis** (19 studies, about 1,800 participants) [4]:
- Chess skill correlated about **r = .24** on average with fluid reasoning, comprehension-knowledge, short-term memory and processing speed.
- Correlations were **stronger for youth (fluid reasoning r ≈ .32) than adults (r ≈ .11)**, and **stronger for unrated/weaker players (r ≈ .32) than rated players (r ≈ .14)**.
- Numerical ability correlated most strongly (r ≈ .35), verbal less (.19) and visuospatial least (.13).

**Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet (2007)** studied 57 young players [5]:
- Practice was the strongest predictor of chess skill, and IQ added explanatory power beyond it.
- In the **elite subsample** (n = 23), IQ did *not* predict skill. Among elite children, those with lower IQ had practised *more*, which compensated.

**Grabner, Stern & Neubauer (2007)**: among adult tournament players, intelligence (especially numerical) explained some variance in Elo beyond experience [7].

**Vaci et al. (2019, PNAS)** analysed long-term rating trajectories with intelligence and practice data. **With equal practice, more intelligent players gained skill faster, peaked higher and declined less** [6]. This is an *interaction* (ability amplifies practice), not a substitute for practice.

**Evidence strength: strong** that a modest positive correlation exists. **Moderate** for the moderators (age, skill level) and for the intelligence × practice interaction.

**Practical meaning:** intelligence matters most early (in children and novices). Among serious, rated players its predictive value is small. It does not prevent anyone from reaching club or expert strength. It probably affects the *rate* at which practice converts into skill.

## 3. Sex differences and participation

**Bilalić et al. (2009)** compared the top 100 male and female players in the German federation database against what would be expected from participation alone. About 96% of the observed rating difference at the top was explained by **men vastly outnumbering women in the participation pool** [8]. This statistical argument does not explain *why* participation differs (culture, stereotype threat, dropout, social environment). Other researchers have contested parts of the model.

**Evidence strength: moderate (contested).**

**Platform implication:** retaining girls and women in the pipeline is a major lever. This connects to the safety, community and dropout design in `../05-children/`.

## 4. Other proposed factors

| Factor | Finding | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Handedness | Chess players are more often left- or mixed-handed than controls (17.9% vs 10.2%), but handedness does **not** predict skill within players [1] | Weak relevance |
| Season of birth | Gobet & Chassy (2008) reported a season-of-birth effect among chess players, but its cause is unclear and its practical relevance is negligible [11] | Weak |
| Working memory | Predicts performance independently of practice in music (Meinz & Hambrick 2010, cited in [9]). In chess it is captured within the cognitive-ability meta-analysis [4] | Moderate |
| Genetics | No large twin study of chess skill was found. In music, a Swedish twin study (Mosing et al. 2014) found practice–ability associations were largely genetically confounded [10]. Applying this to chess is speculative | Weak (for chess) |
| Personality / motivation | Effects appear to work **through** practice amount. Motivated people practise more, rather than personality directly raising skill (Hambrick et al. 2014, discussing Duckworth et al.) [9] | Moderate |

## 5. Age, peak and decline

- Mean age at peak rating in the Charness et al. sample was about 44 (Roring & Charness 2007, as cited in [2]). That sample is dominated by amateurs who keep studying. Elite players typically peak earlier (roughly 30–40).
- **Vaci, Gula & Bilalić (2015)**: in a large database of active players, decline after the peak was moderated by **continued activity** (games played), and initially more able players declined more gently [11]. A ChessBase re-analysis contested parts of their modelling. *Evidence: moderate, contested.*
- Implication for adult learners: decline in middle age is gradual and partly offset by activity. Adult improvement into the 40s–50s is realistic at club level.

## 6. Implications for Grim Chess

1. **Never gate content or "promise" outcomes on aptitude estimates.** The predictive power of cognitive ability for rated players is small [4], and the ethics of labelling learners are poor.
2. **Be honest about base rates.** For an adult starting at 25, reaching GM is a statistical outlier outcome. Club, expert and master levels are realistic with sustained, well-directed work (see `time-to-mastery-evidence.md`).
3. **Encourage early, enjoyable, structured exposure for children** (starting-age evidence), without early specialization pressure (see `../05-children/`).
4. **Track the learning rate per learner** (rating gain per hour of deliberate practice). This empirical, individual estimate is far better for planning than group-level talent predictors, and it reflects the intelligence × practice interaction [6].
5. **Treat retention and dropout as a core metric,** given the participation findings [8].

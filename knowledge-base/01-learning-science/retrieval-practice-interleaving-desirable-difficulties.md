# Retrieval Practice, Interleaving and Desirable Difficulties Applied to Chess

## 1. Learning vs performance

**Soderstrom & Bjork (2015)** distinguish *performance* during training, which is observable and often misleading, from *learning*, the durable change shown later and in new contexts [8]. Conditions that make practice look smooth (massing, blocking, re-reading, immediate hints) often produce weaker learning. Conditions that slow acquisition (spacing, interleaving, testing, generation) often produce stronger retention and transfer. Bjork calls these **desirable difficulties** [8][9].

Two caveats run through the whole literature:
- A difficulty is desirable **only if the learner can overcome it** with existing knowledge. Difficulty without the means to succeed is just failure [9].
- Learners **systematically misjudge** which conditions help them. They prefer the conditions that feel fluent [6][8].

## 2. Retrieval practice (the testing effect)

- **Roediger & Karpicke (2006):** restudying beat testing on an immediate test, but the pattern **reversed at 2 days and 1 week**. Students who were tested recalled more [1].
- **Rowland (2014) meta-analysis:** mean testing-effect size g ≈ 0.50. Effects were larger with feedback and when initial retrieval success was reasonably high [2].
- **Adesope et al. (2017) meta-analysis** (classroom and lab): practice tests outperformed restudy and other comparison conditions, g ≈ 0.61 overall [3].
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing "high utility" [4].

**Evidence strength: strong** (hundreds of studies, mostly verbal and conceptual material).

**Chess translation.** Every puzzle, "guess the move" exercise, recall-a-line card or "what is the plan here?" prompt is retrieval practice. Reading annotated games or watching videos is restudy. Retrieval is best when the learner **commits to a full answer before seeing feedback**.

## 3. Generation, errorful learning and hypercorrection

- **Generation and pretesting.** Attempting to answer *before* being taught, even if the attempt fails, improves later learning of the correct answer, provided corrective feedback follows (Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009) [10].
- **Hypercorrection.** High-confidence errors, once corrected, are more likely to be fixed on later tests than low-confidence errors (Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001) [11].

**Chess translation.** Let learners try a position before the lesson explains it. Ask for a confidence rating on calculation answers. Confident blunders are prime material for targeted feedback and later review.

**Evidence strength: moderate** (robust in lab studies; less studied with complex skills).

## 4. Interleaving

- **Rohrer & Taylor (2007):** students who practised mixed maths problem types did worse during practice but much better on a delayed test than students who practised in blocks [7].
- **Kornell & Bjork (2008):** interleaved exposure to painters' styles improved style identification. **Most participants nevertheless believed blocking had helped them more** [6].
- **Brunmair & Richter (2019) meta-analysis** (59 studies, 238 effect sizes): overall g ≈ 0.42 in favor of interleaving, with **very high heterogeneity** (I² ≈ 77%) [5]. Moderators:
  - Interleaving helps more when **categories are similar to each other** (hard to discriminate) and when items within a category are less similar.
  - It helps more for **complex** material.
  - **Blocking was better for word-list learning** (g ≈ −0.39) [5].

**Mechanism.** Interleaving forces the learner to identify *which* strategy applies, which is exactly what blocked practice removes [5][7].

**Chess translation.** This is directly relevant. In a game nobody announces "this is a knight-fork position." Blocked theme sets ("20 pins") remove the identification step. They are useful for **first teaching** a motif and harmful as the *only* format. Interleaving matters most for **confusable** motifs, such as deflection vs decoy vs overloading, or Philidor vs Lucena structures.

**Evidence strength: moderate** in general; **no chess-specific RCT found**.

## 5. Contextual interference (motor-skill analogue)

Shea & Morgan (1979) found that random practice schedules impaired acquisition but improved retention and transfer of motor tasks, compared with blocked schedules [12]. This is the motor-learning counterpart of interleaving. It supports varying time controls, colors and position types in training.

## 6. Feedback timing

The literature is mixed. Immediate feedback is better for novices and for correcting procedural errors. Slightly delayed feedback can help retention in some verbal tasks. For chess, the practical rule is: **feedback after the learner has committed to a complete answer**, not move-by-move hints during calculation. Novices are the exception and may need immediate move feedback to avoid cognitive overload (see `cognitive-load-theory.md`). *Evidence: moderate, context-dependent.*

## 7. Learner illusions the platform must design around

| Illusion | Evidence | Design response |
|---|---|---|
| "Blocked sets feel like I'm improving" | Kornell & Bjork 2008 [6] | Default to mixed sets after the introduction. Show learners delayed-test data proving the benefit. |
| "Re-watching the video means I know it" | Roediger & Karpicke 2006 [1]; Dunlosky 2013 [4] | Follow every lesson with retrieval questions. Gate "completion" on a retrieval check, not on watch time. |
| "Hints help me learn faster" | Soderstrom & Bjork 2015 [8] | Make hints cost something (reduced score or rating) and delay them. |
| "Easy puzzles at high success rates are good practice" | Bjork's desirable difficulty [9]; FSRS's stability growth is higher at lower R (see spaced-repetition file) | Target roughly 60–80% success for new-material practice (see below). |

**On the target success rate.** No study establishes an optimal success rate for chess puzzles. The 60–80% band is a design default that sits between the testing-effect finding that retrieval should usually succeed [2] and the desirable-difficulty principle [9]. A commonly cited machine-learning result (Wilson et al. 2019, "the eighty-five percent rule" [13]) derives an optimal error rate of about 15% for gradient-based learners. That is an analogy, not human evidence. *Evidence: weak. This parameter needs A/B testing on the platform.*

## 8. Implications for Grim Chess

1. **Commit-before-feedback** everywhere. Puzzles require a full line for calculation items. Lessons open with a question, not an explanation.
2. **Three-phase motif teaching:** (a) blocked introduction (5–10 items), (b) mixed practice with related motifs, (c) fully mixed "real game" puzzle stream with theme labels hidden.
3. **Discrimination sets** for confusable concepts, such as Philidor vs Lucena, opposition vs triangulation, and "sacrifice works vs sacrifice fails" pairs.
4. **Confidence capture** on calculation exercises. Route confident errors to priority review.
5. **Delayed retests.** Schedule re-tests of taught material days later (feeds the spaced-repetition scheduler) and *show learners* their delayed-test performance, to counter fluency illusions.
6. **Measure learning, not performance.** Platform analytics should judge features by delayed retention and rating change, not by in-session accuracy or engagement.

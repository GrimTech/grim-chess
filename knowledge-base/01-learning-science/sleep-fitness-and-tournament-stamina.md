# Sleep, Physical Fitness, Environment and Tournament Stamina

## 1. Sleep

### 1.1 General evidence
- **Sleep supports memory consolidation.** Diekelmann & Born (2010) review evidence that sleep after learning strengthens and reorganizes memories. Slow-wave sleep is linked particularly to declarative consolidation, and REM to some procedural and emotional memory [1]. *Evidence: strong (general).*
- **Sleep deprivation impairs cognition.** A meta-analysis by Lim & Dinges (2010) found the largest effects on vigilance and simple attention. Effects on complex reasoning were smaller but present [11]. *Evidence: strong.*

### 1.2 Chess-specific evidence
- A **Norwegian study (2020)** tracked 14 competitive players for 120 days with wearable sleep monitoring. Players whose FIDE ratings improved had more deep sleep, less REM and lower respiration rate than those whose ratings fell [2]. **Evidence: weak.** The sample is tiny, the result is an association, and multiple comparisons were made.
- No randomized trials of sleep manipulation on chess performance were found.

### 1.3 Practical guidance (inferred from general evidence)
- Learning sessions shortly before normal sleep are not harmful and may help consolidation. Late-night blitz that cuts sleep is likely net-negative.
- Sleep before rest days matters during tournaments. Elite camp reports describe 9–10 hours of sleep for teenage players. Praggnanandhaa's coach reported 9 hours before the 2023 World Cup and about 10 hours in a 2024 Candidates camp [8][9]. *Anecdotal.*

## 2. Physical activity and fitness

### 2.1 Acute exercise
**Chang et al. (2012)**, a meta-analysis of 79 studies, found that acute exercise has **small positive effects on cognitive performance**. Effects varied with timing, intensity, duration and type of cognitive test [3]. Effects were generally largest for tests taken after exercise rather than during it. *Evidence: moderate.*

### 2.2 Chronic exercise
**Ludyga et al. (2020)**, a meta-analysis of long-term exercise interventions in healthy individuals, found small overall improvements in cognition. Moderators included age group and exercise type [4]. *Evidence: moderate. The effects are small, and none of these studies measured chess skill.*

### 2.3 The "6,000 calories" myth
Media reports in 2019 claimed that grandmasters burn up to 6,000 kcal a day during tournaments. The figure appears to be an **unsupported extrapolation** from breathing-rate observations. No metabolic study supports it [6][7]. Measured or estimated values are in the tens of kcal per hour above resting [6]. *Evidence: strong that the 6,000 figure is false.* Tournament play is psychologically stressful, with elevated heart rate and breathing, and players do report weight loss over long events. That is better explained by stress, reduced appetite and disrupted routines than by large energy expenditure.

### 2.4 What elite players report doing
- **Botvinnik** emphasized physical fitness as part of his school's preparation [10].
- **Carlsen** sought advice from the Norwegian Olympic training center in 2017. He keeps tournament-day energy and nutrition routines and plays recreational sport [7].
- **Caruana** reportedly runs, swims and plays basketball as part of stamina training [7].
- **Indian elite camps** combine about 8 hours of chess with 3–4 hours of sport and physical exercise. This was reported for a 2024 Candidates preparation camp [8].

*Evidence: anecdotal.* These routines are consistent with general health and cognition evidence but are not validated for chess outcomes.

## 3. Environment

**Künn, Palacios & Pestel (2023, *Management Science*)** measured indoor air quality in German tournament halls (121 players, three 7-round tournaments, about 30,000 engine-evaluated moves) [5]:
- Higher indoor **PM2.5** concentrations were associated with **more erroneous moves**, and the effect was **larger under time pressure**.
- The design exploited quasi-random variation in pollution across rounds and controlled for player fixed effects.

*Evidence: moderate–strong (careful observational design; one setting).* Implication: ventilation and air filtration at events, and in home study spaces, plausibly matter.

## 4. Tournament stamina

Classical games can last 5–7 hours. Multi-round events run for 9–14 days. Stamina factors with plausible support:
1. **Sleep regularity** across the event (§1).
2. **Aerobic fitness**: small cognitive benefits, plus better recovery and stress tolerance (§2). *Moderate–weak.*
3. **Nutrition and hydration**: stable energy intake. Some players avoid sugar spikes (Carlsen's orange-juice adjustment [7]). *Anecdotal.*
4. **Practice at the target time control.** Endurance for 5-hour concentration is trained by playing long games. Blitz-only practice does not build it. *Expert consensus.*
5. **Between-round recovery.** Light movement, limited post-game engine analysis late at night, and preparation boundaries. Carlsen reportedly preps much less on game day than some peers [7]. *Anecdotal.*

## 5. Implications for Grim Chess

1. **Wellness module, clearly labelled by evidence strength.** Covers sleep (strong general evidence), exercise (moderate, small effects) and environment (moderate). It is never presented as a "secret" to chess strength.
2. **Late-night nudges.** Optionally detect late-night blitz streaks (local time) and suggest stopping, framed as protecting consolidation and next-day performance.
3. **Long-game training blocks** for tournament-oriented learners, with stamina tracking: accuracy over moves 1–30 vs 30–60, and across the hours of a session.
4. **Tournament-prep checklists**: sleep schedule, travel, nutrition, ventilation where controllable, between-round routines.
5. **No medical claims.** Physical-activity guidance follows public-health recommendations, and the platform must not prescribe diets or supplements.

# Chess in Schools and the Transfer Question: An Honest Review

## 1. The claim

Advocates often claim that learning chess improves children's mathematics, reading, concentration, IQ or "thinking skills". This is a claim of **far transfer**: training in one domain improving performance in a different domain. Many school chess programmes and some national policies rest partly on it. Armenia made chess part of the curriculum for grades 2–4, and Hungary later followed [8].

## 2. Evidence timeline

### 2.1 Early studies and the 2006 critical review
Many small studies from the 1970s–2000s reported positive effects. **Gobet & Campitelli (2006)** reviewed them and concluded that the claimed educational benefits were **not established empirically** because of weak designs: no randomization, no active controls and possible selection and placebo effects [1]. They noted possible motivational benefits of compulsory scholastic chess [1][11].

### 2.2 Sala & Gobet (2016) meta-analysis
- 24 studies (40 effect sizes), about 2,788 chess-group and 2,433 control participants [2].
- **Overall g = 0.338.** Mathematics g = 0.382, reading g = 0.248 [2].
- A positive effect of treatment **duration**. Studies with more than about 25 hours showed larger effects (about 0.43) [2].
- **Crucial caveat:** *no* study used an ideal design (pre/post testing, random allocation, both passive and active controls). Almost none controlled for placebo effects [2].

### 2.3 Design quality explains most of the effect
- **Sala & Gobet (2017a)**, three meta-analyses on chess, music and working-memory training in children: effect sizes were **inversely related to design quality**. Studies with active controls showed little or no effect. **"Far transfer rarely occurs"** [3].
- **Sala, Gobet, Trinchero & Ventura (2016):** chess pupils did better in maths than a **Go-course placebo group**, but **not** better than regular-lesson controls. This pattern suggests the comparison group drives the result [5].
- **Sala & Gobet (2017b)**, two experiments with an active control group on mathematical problem-solving, found no meaningful advantage for chess [4].
- **Sala et al. (2019) second-order meta-analysis** of cognitive-training domains (including chess, music, working memory and video games): when placebo effects and publication bias are accounted for, **far-transfer effects are close to zero** [12].

### 2.4 The EEF-funded RCT in England (Jerrim et al. 2016/2018)
- **Design:** a clustered RCT across **100 primary schools**, with **4,009 Year 5 pupils** in 11 local authorities, delivered by Chess in Schools and Communities in 2013/14. The primary outcome was **Key Stage 2 maths about one year after the intervention** [6][7][13].
- **Dosage:** intended 30 hours. Only a third of schools received all 30, and most received 25–29 hours [7]. That is above the about 25-hour threshold suggested by Sala & Gobet (2016) [7].
- **Results** [7]:
  - Maths: effect **essentially zero** (95% CI roughly −0.15 to +0.15 SD). LATE estimate +0.001 SD (CI −0.166 to +0.168).
  - Reading: −0.06 SD (CI −0.21 to +0.09). Science: −0.03 SD (CI −0.13 to +0.08).
  - No benefit for subgroups, including children eligible for free school meals (+0.01, CI −0.18 to +0.19).
- **The authors' explanation** for the earlier positive literature: small samples (the median study had **54 pupils**), problematic testing instruments and testing directly after the intervention. Their simulation shows that a sample of about 54 has about a **25% chance** of producing an effect above 0.2 SD by chance, and that about 400+ pupils are needed to bring false positives below 5% [7].
- **Limitations:** the outcome was measured a year later, so it cannot rule out short-lived immediate effects. The programme was one specific curriculum and delivery model [7]. Critics from the chess side raise dosage and delivery-consistency concerns [6].

### 2.5 Danish quasi-experiment (Rosholm, Mikkelsen & Gumede 2017)
- Replaced one weekly maths lesson in grades 1–3 with chess-based maths instruction. **Non-randomized**, difference-in-differences with extensive controls [9].
- Reported maths gains of about **0.16–0.18 SD**. Effects were concentrated among children who were less engaged or unhappy at school [9].
- **Caveats:** non-random assignment, and the intervention was *maths taught through chess*, not chess alone. That is closer to an alternative maths pedagogy than to far transfer.

### 2.6 Italian studies
Trinchero & Sala (2016) studied about 1,057 Italian children aged 7–11 and reported improvements in maths problem-solving [10]. Jerrim et al. criticize its outcome measure: seven PISA items designed for 15-year-olds [7].

## 3. Where the evidence stands

| Claim | Verdict | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Chess instruction raises academic attainment (maths/reading/science) in the long run | **Not supported** (large RCT null) [7] | Strong |
| Chess raises general intelligence (IQ) | **Not supported** [3][12] | Strong |
| Chess produces small short-term maths gains | Possible in some designs, but mostly with weak or passive controls [2][3][5] | Weak–moderate |
| Chess-integrated maths teaching helps some disengaged pupils | One quasi-experiment [9] | Weak–moderate |
| Chess improves chess skill and chess-related memory | Yes (near transfer, by definition) | Strong |
| Chess is enjoyable, social and a worthwhile pursuit in itself | Plausible. It is a value judgment, not a transfer claim | n/a |

Sala, Foley & Gobet (2017) summarize the field: the "chess effect" hypothesis has some support, but it **has not been convincingly demonstrated**, and far transfer is rare and usually small [11].

## 4. What Grim Chess may and may not claim

**May claim (with citations):**
- "Chess is a rich, enjoyable game that children can get good at, and our training improves chess skill."
- "Some studies have reported short-term maths benefits, but the largest and most rigorous trial found no lasting effect on attainment."

**Must not claim:**
- "Chess makes your child smarter / raises IQ / improves grades."
- "Proven to boost concentration/academic results."
- Any marketing that cites Sala & Gobet (2016)'s g = 0.34 without also citing the design-quality caveat and the RCT null.

## 5. Implications for the children's track

1. Justify chess for children on **intrinsic grounds**: enjoyment, mastery, social connection and competition. Do not justify it on transfer.
2. **For schools,** be transparent and link to this file. Offer curricula without implying academic gains.
3. **Evaluate honestly.** If the platform runs school programmes, partner with independent evaluators. Pre-register, use active controls and use delayed outcomes, following the Jerrim et al. lesson [7].
